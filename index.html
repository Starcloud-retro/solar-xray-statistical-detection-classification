01

Observe

What does GOES actually measure?

GOES does not give us “flares.” It gives us measurements over time. Each row stores when a measurement happened and how much soft X-ray energy the satellite saw at that moment.

The first column is the timestamp in UTC. The second is the flux: X-ray power arriving per square metre, in watts per square metre (W/m²). A new row arrives about once a minute, the instrument’s ~1-minute cadence.

The satellite is GOES-18. Its X-ray sensor, called XRS, watches two wavelength bands. We use XRS-B, the band from 0.1 to 0.8 nanometres, the one NOAA uses to name flare classes.

A flare is just a bump in this line. Its class comes from how high the bump gets: B starts at 10⁻⁷ W/m², C at 10⁻⁶, M at 10⁻⁵.

Why does the raw file show two channels?

The project’s cleaned file stacks both sensor bands in one table: same minute, two rows. Every figure on this page filters it to energy == "0.1-0.8nm" first.

If one row is only one minute, how much data do seven days create?

02

Count

Why are there 10,078 rows, and how many are real measurements?

Seven days of minutes would be 7 × 24 × 60 = 10,080. The file begins at 13:40 on 4 September and its last row is 13:37 on 11 September, so it holds 10,078 timestamp rows, two short of a full seven days.

But a row is not the same thing as a measurement. Every minute has a row, and some rows are empty.

Of the 10,078 rows, 9,536 contain a direct measurement, 1 contains a value we reconstructed, and 541 have no value at all. That leaves 9,537 usable values for everything that follows.

Hover over any dot to see its minute.

What should we do about the 541 empty minutes?

03

Prepare

What do we do with a missing minute?

One minute missing between two good ones. The signal is plainly continuing, so we fill the hole with a straight-line estimate, draw it in teal, and keep a flag in the data (was_imputed = TRUE) so it can never pass as a measurement.

Several minutes missing. Now we would be inventing the shape of what happened, perhaps hiding a flare. So we leave the gap empty and draw no line across it.

The rule is simple: gaps of up to 3 minutes are reconstructed; longer gaps stay empty. Short gap = keep continuity. Long gap = keep the uncertainty.

Why do we use log10?

A flare multiplies the signal; it does not just add to it.

Flux can jump tenfold in minutes. On an ordinary axis, one big flare squashes the quiet background flat and hides everything else. On a logarithmic axis, equal steps mean equal ratios, so a small rise in a quiet hour and a large rise in a flare can be compared fairly.

We write that as log10(flux). The reconstructed minute above has flux 3.872 × 10⁻⁷ W/m², which is log10 = −6.412. From here on, “how fast the signal changed” means the change in log10 per minute.

With clean minutes in hand, what counts as “normal”?

04

Define Normal

Where is the recent background centered?

Where is it centered? Take the last 30 minutes before the orange point. Two honest answers: the mean (add everything up, divide by 30) and the median (the middle value). Now drag the window forward into the flare. A flare can pull the mean upward. The median moves less, so it gives us a second view of the recent background.

How much does it normally vary? The SD (standard deviation) measures how far values typically sit from the mean, and large departures count heavily. MAD asks a simpler question: how far are recent values, typically, from the median? A few extreme minutes barely change it.

Why multiply MAD by 1.4826?

For bell-shaped (Normal) data, the MAD comes out at about 0.67449 of the SD (σ): MAD ≈ 0.67449σ. To put MAD on the same scale as σ we divide by that number, and 1 ÷ 0.67449 ≈ 1.4826.

After the multiplication, “one robust spread” and “one SD” mean roughly the same thing when the background is calm, which lets us compare the two Z-scores fairly.

In the project’s detector the baseline is the trailing 30 minutes, the current minute excluded, with flagged flare minutes kept out. The slider above uses the plain last 30 values so you can watch what a flare does to each statistic. At its default position (19:04 on 4 September) the two are identical.

Given a baseline, how unusual is this minute?

05

Detect

How unusual is this minute?

We ask four questions about every minute. Z-score: how many local spreads above recent normal is it? That is its distance from the recent mean, counted in SDs. Robust Z: the same, but from the median in scaled-MAD units. ROC (rate of change): how fast did log10 flux rise since the last minute?

The fourth question is persistence. One strange minute may be noise; an event has to stay unusual for at least 3 consecutive minutes. When it does, the run of minutes becomes one event with a start, a peak and an end.

The frozen detector also checks a 5-minute slope. Thresholds: Z > 3.0, robust Z > 3.0, ROC > 0.05, persistence ≥ 3.

Does an event we detected correspond to a flare NOAA recognises?

06

NOAA

Does our event correspond to a recognised flare?

NOAA keeps its own list of flares. Alignment means pairing each event we detected with a NOAA record whose peak falls close to ours in time. The zoom above shows one pair: our detector caught the rise of NOAA’s C6.7 flare, and our peak sits 9 minutes before NOAA’s.

The NOAA list gives us two things: a way to check the detector, and the class labels (B, C, M) that the machine-learning step needs.

Overall, the detector finds almost every NOAA flare (high recall) but also reports many events NOAA does not list (low precision). We keep both numbers side by side on purpose.

Unmatched does not mean “false flare.” An unmatched detection may be activity weaker than a B flare, ordinary noise, or something that is simply not on NOAA’s list. We cannot tell which from the NOAA list alone.

Which of these detections become examples for machine learning?

07

Event Dataset

Why does machine learning use only 31 events?

A classifier needs examples with a known answer. Only detections linked to a NOAA record have one, so the 80 unmatched detections cannot teach the model anything about class.

Next, one physical flare must count once. Three NOAA flares had been picked up as two separate detector intervals each. Merging them leaves 32 independent physical events.

One of the 32 has an unspecified class (U) in the NOAA list, so it has no usable label and leaves. Of the remaining 31, the target is B versus C+, where C+ means C or M. That is 13 B events and 18 C+ events.

The dots are drawn schematically, so which three flares were merged is not shown; the counts are the real ones.

What does a single event look like to a model?

08

Features

What does one event look like to the model?

A model cannot look at a curve. It needs a short list of numbers. For every event we measure five things, all taken from the climb between the start and the peak: the background flux before the event, the rise duration in minutes, the rise slope (a straight-line fit through the climb, in log10 units per minute), the max ROC (the single steepest minute), and the mean positive ROC (the average of the minutes that rose).

These five values become one row in the machine-learning table. The curve above is event EVT_0017, a C1.5 flare on 5 September.

What may the model know?

At the moment of the peak, the future decay has not happened yet.

We ask the model to classify an event at the moment its peak is observed, t_pred = t_peak. Anything that happens after the peak is the future and must stay out of the features. That is why both ROC features use only the start-to-peak interval.

This is nowcasting: classifying an event we can already see. It is not forecasting, which would mean predicting a flare before its signal appears.

One feature is excluded for a different reason. peak_flux is not a feature because NOAA’s class is defined by peak X-ray flux. Giving it to the classifier would hand over the answer, a partly circular task.

Stage 8.5 correction: the two ROC features were first computed over the whole event. Restricting them to start-to-peak left max_roc unchanged for all events and changed mean_pos_roc for 2 events.

How does a model turn five numbers into B or C+?

09

Model

How does the model turn five numbers into B or C+?

How was it tested?

Train on the past, test on the future.

Events are sorted by time. The earliest 18 train the models, the next 6 (development) are used to choose between them, and the latest 7 are locked away as the final test. No random shuffling: a model that has seen the future would be cheating.

The main score is Macro-F1: the average of how well the model does on B events and on C+ events, counted equally, so a model cannot look good just by favouring the larger class. Balanced accuracy is a second view of the same idea.

Logistic Regression was selected through the project’s predefined development procedure. The table lists the three models without ranking them: with only one B event in the development set, the differences are a handful of events.

If the final test is 7 out of 7, how much should we trust the patterns?

10

Audit

How trustworthy are the patterns?

Leave one event out, repeatedly

LOOCV (leave-one-out cross-validation) removes one event, trains on the other 30, predicts the removed one, and repeats for all 31. It asks how sensitive the result is to any single event. It is a different question from the chronological test, so the two numbers are not interchangeable.

Are the five features independent?

The three rise-rate features are almost the same measurement. Their scatterplots hug a line, correlations are 0.91 to 0.97, and the VIF (how much a feature is explained by the others; values above roughly 5–10 are a common warning) is about 20–35 for them. The model cannot say which one “matters”.

    Conclusion

    The Sun became data, data became statistics, statistics became events, and events became machine-learning examples.

    Seven days of one X-ray channel were cleaned without inventing data, compared with a robust local baseline, and turned into 115 detected events. NOAA’s records confirm that the detector finds nearly every listed flare, and also show that most of what it reports is unconfirmed. The 31 events that could be labelled became rows of five rise-phase features, taken only from information available at the peak.

    On the chronological test the Logistic Regression classified 7 of 7 events correctly. That is a result on seven events, five B and two C+, and it does not establish generalization. This is a proof of concept that the pipeline is coherent and leak-free, not a validated flare classifier.
